José Ramírez Has Carried Enough. October Requires Help.

By GOAT — a Fourth & Value contributor · 2026-10-05

There is a difference between building around a great player and asking him to cover everything you haven’t built.

For years, José Ramírez has made that distinction uncomfortable for Cleveland.

He supplies power. He steals bases. He manufactures opportunities in lineups that too often struggle to create their own. His excellence can make an offense look more complete than it actually is. Then October arrives, and every empty inning becomes harder to explain.

Cleveland’s 3–0 loss to the White Sox in this year’s American League Division Series opener brought that concern back into focus. Chicago held the Guardians to three hits and struck out 12. One game cannot settle an argument about an organization’s roster construction. It can, however, show how little room an offense has when almost nothing is working. [1]

The question for Cleveland is how many different hitters can change a postseason game—and whether too much of that expectation still falls on Ramírez.

The historical evidence explains why fans keep looking his way.

In 2022, Ramírez drove in 126 runs. Josh Naylor had 79. Andrés Giménez had 69. RBI totals depend partly on who reaches base ahead of a hitter, but that was still an enormous difference in production among Cleveland’s key bats. [2]

In 2024, Ramírez hit 39 home runs, stole 41 bases and finished with an .872 OPS. In 2025, he followed with a .283 average, 30 home runs, 44 steals and an .863 OPS. Cleveland could turn to the same player for a home run or a stolen base, depending on what the inning demanded. [3]

But 2025 offers the clearest picture of the burden.

Cleveland finished 28th in runs per game and 29th in OPS. That included Ramírez’s production. [4]

Calculations from the published season totals show that he accounted for approximately 11.3% of Cleveland’s plate appearances, yet supplied 17.9% of its home runs and 34.1% of its stolen bases. While he hit .283, everyone else combined hit approximately .219. That last number describes the other hitters’ actual results; it is not a projection of what Cleveland would have done with a replacement in his spot. [3] [5] [6]

Those figures help explain the familiar feeling that Cleveland is waiting for José to do something.

Sometimes, he delivers exactly the moment everyone needs.

On September 18, 2025, Ramírez hit a tiebreaking, two-run homer in the seventh inning against Detroit. Cleveland won 3–1 and completed a three-game sweep of the division leaders. It was the kind of swing that makes a star feel indispensable. [7]

It also creates a dangerous expectation. Once a player has rescued enough games, another rescue can begin to feel like the plan.

To be fair, Cleveland has supplied real help. Its pitching staff finished 2025 with a 3.70 ERA, fourth-best in baseball. Kyle Manzardo hit 27 home runs. Those contributions matter, and describing the Guardians as Ramírez surrounded by passengers would shortchange the people who helped them win. [6]

The concern is offensive depth. Good pitching can keep a game close for nine innings. Someone still has to score.

And the argument becomes more urgent when Ramírez himself struggles.

His 2026 regular season produced a .235 average, 12 home runs and a .688 OPS—a substantial decline from the previous two years. His history earns respect, but those numbers belong in any honest assessment of Cleveland’s current offense. He has to contribute, too. [3]

That is precisely why an organization needs more than the expectation that its best player will eventually look like himself.

In a short postseason series, a hitter may get only a few meaningful chances to influence the result. An opposing pitcher can work around him when the situation allows. A good swing can become an out. A cold stretch can last longer than the team’s October.

A deeper lineup creates more opportunities for somebody else to deliver. It gives a struggling star room to recover and makes an opponent pay for avoiding him.

Cleveland deserves credit for reaching October. The harder standard is building an offense with enough answers to keep playing there.

Ramírez has repeatedly shown what he can give this franchise. The postseason should also reveal what the franchise has put around him.

He has carried enough innings, enough rallies and enough expectations. If Cleveland wants a long October, more of the lineup has to help write it.
