Sportsbooks priced Wild Card strikeout props for October’s quick hooks. The hooks came—but pitchers struck out batters so fast that the overs won anyway.

Updated Sep 30, 2026 · 6 completed Wild Card games · 11 starting pitchers

It’s early. This is 11 starts from the first 6 games of the postseason. One dominant outing can swing a sample this small, so treat this as what we are seeing so far, not a law of October. We will keep updating it as the playoffs go on.

Every strikeout prop has a favorite: the side that costs more than it pays—say, Under 7.5 at −140, where you risk $140 to win $100. Books shade that price toward the outcome they think is more likely. So far this October they have favored the under on 7 of 11 starters. Those unders have won 2.

Every starter, at a glance

Read each row like a target. The blue zone is the side the books favored (with their implied chance, e.g. “Under 53%”); the white tick is the line; the orange dot is how many batters the pitcher struck out. A dot outside the blue zone means the favorite lost.

The prices were more confident than the results

On average, the books’ prices gave their favored side about a 55% chance. That side won 36% of the time. When they leaned under, the gap was wider still.

Nobody landed near the line

A strikeout line is the market’s idea of the middle. In these games starters finished an average of 2.2 strikeouts away from it—and on balance, +0.6 above it.

The October explanation: hooks, and harder-throwing starters

Here is the logic the under-leaning prices suggest, and it is reasonable: in the playoffs, managers pull starters early and lean on rested bullpens. Fewer innings should mean fewer strikeouts.

Half of that came true. 6 starters recorded five innings or fewer. But look at what happened when they were in the game:

Every one of the 5 starters whose strikeout rate jumped went over his line. Sean Burke struck out 8 of the 16 batters he faced; Cam Schlittler struck out 10 of 22. One plausible reason: a starter who expects to face the lineup only twice can throw max effort on every pitch. Even among the 6 short starts, 3 still went over—an early hook is no guarantee of an under when the pitcher is piling up strikeouts while he is in.

The aces got the other half of the October treatment. Michael King, Tyler Mahle, Chris Sale and Cam Schlittler all went at least as deep as usual, because managers ride their best arm when it is working. A long leash plus a higher strikeout rate is how Schlittler beat a 7.5 line by two and a half.

Was the market wrong?

Possibly—but 11 starts can’t prove it. What we can say is that the market’s October story has so far been half right: it appears to have priced in the early hooks, but not the strikeout surges that came with several of them. If that pattern continues into the Division Series, the under’s extra cost is paying for a risk that hasn’t been showing up, and the cheaper over side deserves a long look.

Full disclosure: our own model leaned the same way. 9 of its 11 Wild Card strikeout picks were unders, mostly at minus odds, and they went 5–6. We are reviewing how it handles playoff starters and will report back.

What to watch: whether short starts keep producing strikeout surges, and whether books respond by lowering under prices or raising lines on the aces.
