Why our playoff strikeout favorites keep losing

We graded every Wild Card strikeout prop our model picked. The short version: we kept paying extra to bet unders, and October pitchers kept refusing to land in the middle.

Updated Sep 30, 2026 · Wild Card games through Sep 30 · 11 settled picks

“Favorite” in a strikeout prop means the side that costs more than it pays—say, Under 7.5 at −140, where you risk $140 to win $100. 7 of our 11 picks were favorites, and 9 of 11 were unders. That combination is where the losses came from.

Every pick, at a glance

Read each row like a target. The blue zone is the result we needed. The orange dot is how many batters the pitcher actually struck out. Dot inside the zone: we won.

The math problem with laying juice

When you bet a −137 favorite (our average), you have to win about 58% of the time just to break even. The model said our favorites would win 64% of the time. They actually won 43%.

Seven bets is a tiny sample, and three of the four losses missed by just one or two strikeouts. On its own this would be bad luck. The problem is that the model has done this before.

The model was overconfident last October, too

Before this postseason started, we tested the strikeout model on the 2025 playoffs using a version trained only on data from before them. Its regular-season forecasts were well calibrated. Its playoff forecasts were not: the more certain it sounded, the more it overstated its chances.

That gap is exactly what hurts a favorite-heavy strategy. A 64% call that is really a 55% call does not clear a −137 price. The model flagged it as a 3%+ edge; in reality it was a losing bet.

October pitchers don’t land in the middle

Sportsbook lines and our model both aim for a pitcher’s typical night. October has produced two kinds of starts instead: aces who went deep and missed more bats than usual, and back-end starters who got pulled the moment trouble started.

Our picks were mostly unders on the aces—Chris Sale, Cam Schlittler, Michael King—precisely the pitchers whose managers let them work. Schlittler struck out 10 against a 7.5 line; Sale struck out 9. The early hooks did pay us—Payton Tolle and Brown both cashed unders—but at −134 and −154 those wins returned less than the aces’ overs took away.

The model’s projections are squeezed together

Our model projected these starters between 3.9 and 6.9 strikeouts. Their actual results ranged from 2 to 10. A forecast that cautious will keep calling “under” on any ace with a high line, because it rarely believes in a 9- or 10-strikeout night.

Two starters even received effectively identical projections despite very different profiles: Payton Tolle and Cam Schlittler were both projected at 6.9; Hunter Brown and Sean Burke were both at 5.9. That is a sign the model is falling back on a coarse average rather than distinguishing the pitchers.

Two process mistakes we own

  • We picked both sides of Matthew Boyd. Early on the model liked Under 3.5 at +128. After the price moved, it liked Over 3.5 at +101. Both appeared on the board. One had to lose.
  • We doubled up on Tyler Mahle. Under 4.5 and Under 5.5 were picked in different refreshes. That is one opinion counted twice, and both lost when he struck out 6.

What we are changing, and what you should do

For readers: treat our strikeout picks at minus odds as research, not a bet, for the rest of October. If you like an under on an ace, compare it to the plus-money over first—the market already leans under on most starters, so the under is rarely the bargain.

  • Shrink playoff confidence. We will discount postseason strikeout probabilities toward the market until the model shows it is calibrated in October.
  • Stricter bar for favorites. A minus-odds pick will need a larger edge than a plus-money pick, because overconfidence costs more when you are laying the price.
  • One opinion per pitcher per game. No flipping sides and no stacking alternate lines across refreshes.

Eleven settled picks cannot prove a model is broken. They are, however, consistent with a known weakness, and that is enough to change how we present these picks.
