Modeling in sports betting isn’t about a machine that knows the answer. It’s about a haystack too big for any person to search. Here is how big ours gets, sport by sport, and how a model narrows it to a few bets worth a closer look, as one voice among several.

Oct 8, 2026 · Opinion from Fourth & Value · 9 min read

At 7:07 a.m. Eastern on Sunday, Oct. 4, our morning data jobs finished. The NFL board held 16,916 sportsbook prices for 14 games. Five NHL games added 1,286 more, and two MLB Division Series games another 1,018.

Nobody can look at all of that. We can’t either. That, in one sentence, is our case for modeling in sports betting, and it isn’t the case you usually hear.

The usual pitch is that a model knows something the market doesn’t. On a narrow question it sometimes might. But the more useful thing a model does for us is humbler: it tells us where to look. Modeling and data automation are how we pull a few needles out of a very large haystack. They are not how we decide a needle is sharp.

These are undercounts. We carry a deliberately narrow slice of what books offer: mostly main lines rather than full alternate ladders, no live betting, no same-game parlays, no futures, and only the player markets we have a reason to track. A sportsbook’s own menu for the same games is far larger.

Then basketball arrives

The NBA season starts Oct. 20, and the second night already has 11 games listed in our feed. Our NBA board launched this fall, so we don’t have archived NBA boards from last season to count. Instead, here is the arithmetic, using last season’s schedule and the markets our NBA feed requests.

How we estimated the NBA. Last season had 1,230 regular-season games, about seven or eight on a typical night. Our NBA feed requests 11 player markets per game (points, rebounds, assists, threes, the four combinations, blocks, steals and turnovers) plus moneylines, spreads and totals.

Suppose books post the eight scoring and combination markets for about 16 rotation players a game, and blocks, steals and turnovers for about eight. That’s roughly 150 player questions per game. On our other boards, each question produced about two distinct bets (2.0 in NHL, 2.2 in MLB, 2.5 in NFL) and each bet two to three prices. Call it about 330 distinct bets and 800 prices a game, or about 2,500 distinct bets and 6,000 prices on a seven- or eight-game night. A 12-game night would be closer to 4,000 bets. We’ll replace this estimate with counts from our own board after opening week.

Basketball doesn’t replace anything; it stacks. From late October to early February, a single Sunday can hold a full NFL slate, an NBA slate and a hockey slate. Using the numbers above, that’s plausibly 9,000 to 10,000 distinct bets in one day, before counting a single alternate line.

The arithmetic of attention

Give each distinct bet 30 seconds. That’s barely enough to read the line, glance at the player’s last few games and check one injury report. At that pace one NFL Sunday takes almost 48 hours. A full MLB Friday takes more than 21. A seven-game NHL Tuesday takes more than 11, and an ordinary NBA night about 21. The Oct. 4 morning, all three sports together, comes to more than 56 hours. And 30 seconds isn’t analysis. It’s a skim.

So everyone filters. The only question is whether you filter on purpose. Without a model, the filter is habit: your team, the prime-time game, the star you watched last night. In our view, those are the markets with the most eyes on them, which makes them the least likely place to find a price that’s off. That is an opinion, not something we have measured. If a number is off by half a point, we’d expect to find it in a backup running back’s carries, a fifth starter’s outs or a third-line winger’s shots: places nobody browses on purpose.

The haystack is the reason. Our model doesn’t have to be smarter than the market to be useful. It has to look everywhere, the same way, every morning, and point.

What the model actually does for us

  • Puts every bet on one scale. Each prop gets a projected quantity and, where the model is fitted, a probability we can compare with the price once the book’s margin is removed. Thousands of bets become one sortable list.
  • Does the bookkeeping nobody wants to do by hand. It matches the identical line across up to 11 books, pairs over with under to strip out the margin, drops stale quotes and started games, and finds the best price. Automation makes collecting the haystack cheap: that whole MLB Friday came back from 17 requests to our odds provider. Reading it is the expensive part.
  • Flags disagreement. When a projection sits far from the line, or one book’s price sits far from the others, that is a reason to look. It is not a conclusion.
  • Says what it can’t do. On Oct. 4, 8,518 of the 15,992 NFL prop prices had no calibrated model at all: touchdown-scorer markets, longest-reception lines and players with too little history. Another 680 NFL forecasts were withheld because they failed our reliability or sensitivity checks. A model that knows its blind spots narrows the search honestly.

One Sunday, from haystack to card

Here is what the Oct. 4 morning edition of Today’s Picks did with those 19,220 prices.

  1. 19,220Prices on the morning boards6,775 distinct bets on 674 players, across 21 NFL, NHL and MLB games.
  2. 546Offers passed a model screen475 NFL, 67 NHL and 4 MLB. Another 680 NFL forecasts were held back as unreliable.
  3. 22Got a full reviewDated reporting, injuries, expected opportunity, the price at other books, and a written case and countercase.
  4. 9Made the cardSix NFL and three MLB, about one in every 750 of the morning’s distinct bets.

Look at the third step. Even after the model cut 19,220 prices to 546 offers, there were more candidates than we could read: the review stage has a daily limit, and it reached that limit in both NFL and NHL, leaving 524 screened offers unreviewed. That’s why ranking matters as much as screening. The model doesn’t just say “these are interesting.” It says “start here.”

Alongside the model, a separate AI-assisted search that never sees our model’s numbers was sent all 21 games to look for opportunity, matchup and participation questions. Sent is not the same as exhaustively researched: one bounded request can’t read every market or report. But it is a second, independent way into the haystack, and in our process a researched lead can reach review without the model’s approval. And some mornings the answer is none. The process is allowed to publish an empty card.

Which rules applied. The Oct. 4 card ran under our earlier selection policy. Since Oct. 5, a model candidate must also survive a blend with the market’s price (below). The model is one voice. Here are the others.

When a candidate reaches review, the model’s number starts an argument; it doesn’t settle one. Before anything reaches the card, these get a say:

  • The market. Up to 11 books’ prices, with the margin removed, are a model too, built by people with far more money on the line than we have. Since Oct. 5, when our model and the books’ consensus at that exact line disagree, the consensus gets 75 percent of the weight in the final probability. That weight is a starting value we still have to test, but the direction is deliberate.
  • Independent research. The search that never sees our numbers, so it can’t simply agree with them.
  • Dated reporting. Injury reports, lineups, starting goalies and weather, saved with timestamps. A model trained through yesterday doesn’t know about this morning’s scratch.
  • Opportunity and context. Minutes, snaps, pitch counts and line deployment, the things a season average blurs.
  • The rules. Two books can grade the “same” bet differently. See same bet, different rules.
  • The price. A good read at a bad number is a bad bet. See the edge was the price.

Take Daniel Jones on Oct. 4. Our NFL model projected 35.3 passing attempts against a line of 31.5. The review marked it worth considering, but wrote down where the cushion came from: his recent volume blended to 31.6 attempts, right on the line, and the opponent adjustment lifted it to 35.3. “The adjustment, rather than baseline volume, creates the cushion.” That tells you which assumption to doubt first. He threw 34. We chose this example for the review note, not the result; one ticket proves nothing.

Sometimes the other voices win. When Chris Sale’s five-start average pointed over his pitcher-outs line, the shape of his starts and a bullpen plan our model couldn’t see pointed under. That story is in his average said over, the shape of his starts said under.

What we’re not claiming

  • Our models are experimental. None has been shown to beat betting prices over time, and a well-calibrated forecast is not the same as a profitable one. See our research on Brier scores and calibration.
  • When our model disagrees with the market, the likeliest explanation is that our model is wrong. That’s why disagreement starts a review instead of ending one.
  • A signal is not an edge. We publish the card, the reasoning and the weekly scorecards so you can check our work. Whether to bet is your call.

A metal detector doesn’t dig

Arguments about sports betting models tend to ask whether models “work,” as if the model were the bettor. For us it’s closer to a metal detector. On a morning with 19,220 prices, it sweeps the whole field, beeps a few hundred times and ranks the beeps. Then people, and a research process built to disagree with the model, kneel down and dig. Most of the time it’s a bottle cap. Once in a while it’s worth keeping.

That’s the job: not knowing everything, but knowing where to look.
