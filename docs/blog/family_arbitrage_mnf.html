We now scan the entire market to check for coherence, grouping related player-prop families to identify lines that simply don't add up — cases where one stat (like receptions) implies an efficiency that makes the paired stat (like yards) look unlikely.

By analyzing the market as a whole rather than in isolation, bettors can uncover where sportsbooks have contradicted their own math — revealing possible mispricings before the books correct themselves.

🧠 What's New in the Model

As of October 2025, Fourth & Value's player-prop engine models related markets as families instead of isolated bets.

Each family shares a set of latent factors — such as volume, efficiency, and scoring rate — that keep projections internally consistent:

When a sportsbook moves one line (say, receptions) but doesn't move the other (receiving yards), the implied efficiency can fall outside those bounds. Our model never allows that. Each player's projection must satisfy all related markets simultaneously.

⚙️ Why It Matters

Traditional prop markets — and even most analytics models — treat every stat line independently. One trader adjusts rushing attempts for volume, another adjusts rushing yards for matchup. Over time, those independent adjustments drift apart.

Family-based modeling fixes that by tying each market to its natural counterpart. If a back is projected for 14 carries and 65 yards, that's 4.6 YPC — realistic. If a book posts 14 carries and 95 yards (6.8 YPC), that's far outside a typical game. We catch that instantly, and it's often where inefficiencies appear.

This coherence layer isn't about predicting better; it's about keeping the math real and revealing when books fail to do the same.

🏈 Case Study: Isiah Pacheco's Receiving Lines

As of 2:09 PM ET on Oct 6, 2025, six major sportsbooks — BetMGM, BetOnline, Bovada, DraftKings, FanDuel, and William Hill — are offering the following:

  • Receptions: O/U 1.5
  • Receiving Yards: O/U 6.5

Do the math: 6.5 ÷ 1.5 = 4.33 yards per reception.

Running backs usually average about 6 to 9 yards per catch over a season, so 4.3 looks low. (Corrected: the original said 6 to 18, which is a wide receiver’s range.) But for a player who may catch only one or two passes, dividing one line by the other is only a rough screen: a prop line is closer to a midpoint than an average, and the chance of zero catches pulls the yards line down. Our structural arbitrage explainer shows how to compare the probabilities instead.

💡 The Structural Edge

The original version of this post pointed out that both of these bets could win:

  • Bet Over 1.5 receptions (books expect 2+)
  • Bet Under 6.5 yards (books expect < 7)

That is true: two catches for five yards cashes both. But it is not arbitrage. Arbitrage means a profit whatever happens, and this pair can also lose twice: one catch for 10 yards loses both. Each leg also carries the vig.

What happened: Pacheco caught 3 passes for 20 yards in Kansas City’s 31–28 loss. The receptions over won and the yards under lost, a common way for this pair to split, because two or more catches usually means more than 6.5 yards. In this game the yards line was the one that proved too low.

🔍 What It Means for Bettors

When linked lines look inconsistent, check the probabilities, not just the ratio. Two bets that can both win can usually both lose too, so an inconsistency is a reason to look harder at one side, not a guaranteed edge.

Family-based modeling gives us the visibility to spot these mismatches the moment they appear. We still model performance and probability, but this added layer shows when the books have priced themselves into a corner.

🧭 Conclusion

Family-based modeling does two things:

  1. It keeps every projection grounded in real-world efficiency.
  2. It surfaces opportunities when sportsbooks don't follow their own math.

The Pacheco case shows how independent line-setting can produce lines that look inconsistent. A coherence check finds them; deciding which side, if either, is mispriced still takes probability, price and context.

As sportsbooks get sharper, these gaps will close. Until then, our coherence checks will keep flagging when the numbers simply don't add up, and that's where to start looking for value.
