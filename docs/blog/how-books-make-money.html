How Sportsbooks Make Money (and Why We Devig)

Sportsbooks aren’t crystal balls. They don’t know the outcome of a game any more than you do. What they do know is how to set prices so that, over many bets, they expect to make a profit. That profit comes from a built-in fee called the vig (short for “vigorish,” also called the “juice”).

What is the Vig?

The vig is the sportsbook’s commission. It’s why a standard point spread or total bet is often priced at -110 instead of true “even odds” (+100).

Take a coin flip — 50/50 in reality. If a sportsbook priced both sides fairly, you’d see +100 on heads and +100 on tails. Bet $100, win $100. But that wouldn’t make them money. Instead, they list both at -110:

Heads:  -110  (implies ~52.38%)
Tails:  -110  (implies ~52.38%)

Do the math: 52.38% + 52.38% = 104.76%. That extra 4.76% is the overround, the margin built into the prices. In dollars, it works out a little smaller: if the book takes $110 on each side, it collects $220 and pays the winner $210, keeping $10. That is about 4.5% of the money wagered, which is the book’s hold.

That $10 is only guaranteed when the money is balanced. If most of the bets land on one side, the book is exposed to that result and can lose. That is one reason lines move.

Why Raw Odds Don’t Tell the Whole Story

Those -110 prices make it look like each side is more likely than it really is. In truth, the game is still a 50/50 coin flip — the book just built in a margin. If you read raw odds as probabilities, you’ll overestimate every outcome slightly.

What Does “Devigging” Mean?

To level the playing field, we devig the odds. Devigging means stripping out the sportsbook’s margin to reveal the underlying probabilities the odds imply.

In the coin flip example:

Raw odds:
  -110 = 52.38% each side

Sum = 104.76% (includes vig)

Devigged odds:
  52.38 / 104.76 = 50.0% each side

Now you’re back to reality: 50% vs 50%.

This is the proportional method, the simplest and most common way to devig. When the two prices are lopsided, such as a heavy favorite against a longshot, other methods split the margin differently. They usually agree within a percentage point or two on everyday spreads and totals.

Why We Show Market Probability on Fourth & Value

If you’ve looked at our Player Props boards, you’ll see two probabilities side by side:

  • Market probability — the devigged, fair probability implied by the sportsbook’s line.
  • Model probability — our own estimate of the true probability.

Without devigging, you’d be comparing apples to oranges — our “fair” model vs the book’s “padded” prices. By removing vig, we can show a cleaner head-to-head comparison.

Takeaway

The vig is how sportsbooks keep the lights on. But once you understand it, you can strip it away and see the real picture. That’s why we always devig, and why our boards compare the market’s probability with the model’s.
