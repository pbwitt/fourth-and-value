Sharp vs. Soft Sportsbooks: How Pinnacle Shapes the Odds

Analysis, not a guarantee. Picks and projections are uncertain; odds and player information can change. No outcome or profit is guaranteed. Any wager is your decision and responsibility. Read our limitations and responsible-play guidance.

Every sportsbook posts a price, but not every price carries the same information. A few books set their numbers by taking large bets from people who are good at this, and they move quickly when they are wrong. Many others start from those numbers and add a bigger margin. Knowing which is which tells you whose price to trust, and it is why so many bettors check Pinnacle before they bet anywhere else.

How a betting market gets made

  1. Someone posts a first number. A market-making book opens a line from its own models and traders. The first number is the least tested, so it comes with low limits.
  2. Bettors test it. Bets from people with a good read, professionals included, push the price. A market maker wants that action: each sharp bet tells it where the number should be.
  3. The price firms up and limits rise. As the line survives more money and more news, the book trusts it more and lets people bet more. By the start of the game it reflects most public information, which is why the closing line is the usual scorecard. Our guide to opening and closing lines explains why.
  4. Other books follow. Many books do not run this process for every game. They start from a market maker’s number or a trading supplier’s, adjust it for their own customers, take a larger margin and keep limits lower. Some limit or close accounts that win consistently.

Pinnacle is one of the books near the start of that chain. Other operators watch its prices, and it also sells trading and risk-management services to other sportsbooks through its business-to-business arm, Pinnacle Solution. So yes, Pinnacle shapes many of the prices you see elsewhere. It is not the only source: other market makers, betting exchanges and books with their own traders set prices too, and many player props have no single price leader.

Sharp books and soft books

Sharp (market-making) bookSoft (retail) book
Where the price comes fromIts own trading, shaped by sharp betsOften a market maker’s or supplier’s number, then adjusted
MarginLow on main marketsHigher, especially on props and parlays
LimitsHigh on main lines once the number firms upLower
Winning customersWelcomed: their bets improve the priceOften limited
BonusesFew or noneSign-up offers, boosts, promotions

The margin difference is easy to see. With −110 on both sides, each side implies 52.38% and the book keeps 4.55% of balanced action. At −105 on both sides, each side implies 51.22% and the book keeps 2.38%. Cutting the margin in half roughly halves what it costs to have an opinion. Our guide to the vig shows the math.

−110 / −110 → 52.38% each, hold 4.55%
−105 / −105 → 51.22% each, hold 2.38%

Soft doesn’t mean bad. A book that is slow to move, or that shades a price toward what its customers like to bet, is exactly where you find a good number. The sharp price tells you whether you found one.

Pinnacle in brief

  • Low margins instead of bonuses. Pinnacle began taking bets in 1998 and built its business on low margins rather than sign-up offers.
  • Winners welcome. It is known for taking large bets and for not limiting winning bettors, because their money helps it find the right price.
  • Not available in the United States. It stopped taking bets from U.S. customers in January 2007, after the Unlawful Internet Gambling Enforcement Act became law. It has been licensed in Ontario since 2022.
  • Sharpest on main lines. Its strongest prices are moneylines, spreads and totals in major leagues. Player props and smaller markets carry lower limits at most books, so a prop price, even Pinnacle’s, has been tested by less money.
Only bet with operators licensed where you live. You don’t need an account to use a sharp price as a reference: odds services publish it, and that is how we record it.

Using a sharp price to check yours

A sharp book’s listed price still includes its margin. Remove the margin first, then compare with your book’s price at the exact same line. Here is a made-up NHL example, a player’s Over 0.5 points:

Sharp bookPriceImpliedNo-vig
Over 0.5 points+14041.67%39.57%
Under 0.5 points−17563.64%60.43%
Total105.30%100.00%

The sharp market’s fair price for the Over is about +153. Now compare two prices your book might offer:

Your book’s OverBreak-evenExpected value at 39.57%
+15040.00%−1.1%
+16038.46%+2.9%

+150 beats the sharp book’s +140 and is still a losing bet against the sharp no-vig chance. Beating a sharp book’s listed price isn’t enough; you have to beat its fair price. Before you trust the comparison, check four things:

  • Same bet. The same line and the same settlement rules. See our NFL, NBA, MLB and NHL rules guides.
  • Same time. Compare prices taken close together. A sharp price from this morning may have moved since.
  • Main lines. Sharp books mostly post one main line. An alternate line at your book has no direct sharp comparison.
  • Thin markets. A prop the sharp book barely trades is weaker evidence than a main spread.

Our models are experimental, and the betting market is hard to beat. Before any model idea can be a pick, we blend its chance with the market’s no-vig chance at the exact same line.

Frequently asked questions

What is a sharp sportsbook?

A sharp, or market-making, sportsbook sets its own prices and lets bets from winning players move them. It runs low margins and high limits on main markets and welcomes winning bettors, because their bets help it find the right price.

What is the difference between sharp and soft sportsbooks?

Sharp books make the market. Soft, or retail, books often start from a market maker’s number, take a larger margin, keep limits lower and may limit winning customers. Soft books are where bettors most often find prices that lag the market.

Why do bettors use Pinnacle’s odds?

Pinnacle runs low margins and takes large bets from winning players, so its prices, especially its closing prices on main lines, are widely treated as the best available estimate of a fair price. Removing the vig from its odds gives a benchmark to compare with other books.

Can you bet with Pinnacle in the United States?

No. Pinnacle stopped accepting U.S. customers in January 2007 and is not available in the United States. Bettors there can still use its published odds as a reference.
