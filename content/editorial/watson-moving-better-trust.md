# Watson Is Moving Better. Can Cleveland Trust Where He’s Going?

*By Timmy Iceman, Fourth & Value contributor*

I think a turnover will define this game. A late throw over the middle. A ball knocked loose while Deshaun Watson tries to escape one more defender. One possession that turns a competitive night into another argument about Cleveland’s quarterback.

That is my concern heading into the Pittsburgh game. Watson looks more mobile than I expected coming back from his Achilles injury. To my eye, he is moving as well as he has during his time with the Browns.

But moving better and playing better are different things. The question is what he does with the extra time his legs create.

Watson still leaves me wondering whether he knows when a play is over. There is a point when extending a possession becomes endangering it. The receiver hasn’t separated. The pocket has collapsed. The defense has won the down. Sometimes the best available decision is to throw the ball away and preserve the next opportunity.

Too often, watching Watson feels like waiting for him to accept that.

He is not Michael Vick. Escaping the first defender does not mean he can outrun every consequence. Mobility gives a quarterback options, but those options are only valuable if he chooses well.

The numbers require some fairness here. Through three games, Watson has five touchdown passes and one interception. His [official game log](https://www.clevelandbrowns.com/team/players-roster/deshaun-watson/logs/) lists one fumble, which Cleveland recovered. Calling this season a parade of turnovers would be inaccurate. Against Tampa Bay, he completed 24 of 30 passes for 238 yards and two touchdowns without an interception. That performance deserves credit.

But the Carolina win showed why a clean interception column does not settle the argument. Watson threw an [end-zone interception that was erased by a roughing-the-passer penalty](https://www.reuters.com/sports/nfl/deshaun-watson-guides-browns-comeback-win-over-panthers--flm-2026-09-27/) before returning to deliver the winning touchdown. The touchdown counts. The interception does not. Both belong in an honest evaluation of how close Cleveland came to a different ending.

That sequence captures my frustration with Watson. There is enough ability to produce a winning moment, and enough uncertainty to make every broken play uncomfortable.

The broader disappointment is how far the conversation has fallen. Cleveland’s quarterback discussion should be about the advantages Watson creates. Instead, I find myself hoping he makes the routine decision, protects the ball and gives the rest of the team a chance.

Those are reasonable expectations for any quarterback. They are a painfully modest definition of success for the player who was supposed to solve the position.

A comeback victory matters. So does showing he can still move after a serious injury. But neither should erase the demand for consistent, dependable quarterback play. Fans can acknowledge improvement without treating every encouraging afternoon as proof that the larger problem has been solved.

Against Pittsburgh, my worry is that Watson will try to rescue a play that no longer deserves saving. A sack can end a drive. A desperate throw or an exposed football can change the game.

That is a prediction, not a certainty. He can prove it wrong by taking the easy completion, securing the ball and recognizing when the defense has earned a stop.

Watson’s legs may give him another chance on a broken play. Cleveland needs his judgment to tell him whether that chance is worth taking.
