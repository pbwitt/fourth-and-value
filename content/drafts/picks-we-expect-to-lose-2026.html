A pick is not a prediction

This is the single most useful idea in betting, and it takes about thirty seconds to absorb.

When our board says Baltimore −1.5, it is not saying Baltimore will win by two. It is saying the price being offered on Baltimore winning by two is too long. Those are different claims. The first is about baseball. The second is about a number somebody typed into a sportsbook.

You can be confident about the second while being genuinely unsure about the first. Most of the time, that is exactly the situation.

What a run line actually asks

A run line is baseball's version of a point spread, and it is almost always set at 1.5 runs. Take the favorite at −1.5 and they have to win by two or more. A one-run win, the most common margin in baseball, loses your bet.

Because that is a demanding ask, the payout is generous. Here are the four the model flagged:

So yes: we expect to lose this bet. We expect to lose it 59 times out of 100. We also think it pays too well to skip.

The model is not calling for blowouts

Here is the part that surprises people. You would assume a model betting four favorites at −1.5 has decided those teams are about to run away with it. It hasn't.

Its projected margins for those games are +0.14 runs for Baltimore, +0.19 for Boston and +0.31 for Philadelphia. In plain terms: it thinks all three games are basically coin flips that lean slightly one way.

What it actually believes is something narrower and less exciting — that baseball scores are more spread out than the run-line prices suggest. Games that look close on paper produce four-run finals all the time: a bullpen gives out in the seventh, a two-out error turns into three runs, a tired starter leaves a fastball up. None of that is predictable in a specific game. Across many games it is perfectly predictable, and it is what the model is quietly pricing.

That is the whole opinion. Not "Baltimore is great." Just "close games break open more often than +173 implies."

Six picks, or one opinion six times?

Honestly? Closer to the second, and this is the caveat we would want a reader to take away.

Six of the sixteen picks are run lines. They are not six independent discoveries. They are one view about how baseball scoring is distributed, applied to six different games on the same night. If that view is right, several will land together. If it is wrong, they will fail together.

Betting all six is therefore much closer to making one large wager than six small ones. Anyone sizing these as six separate positions is taking on more risk than the count suggests. We would rather say that plainly than let a tidy-looking list imply diversification it does not have.

How to read the record afterward

Expect this night to look bad. With four bets at roughly 41%, the single most likely outcome is that one or two of them hit. Going 1–3 is completely ordinary. It is also, at these prices, roughly break-even.

This is where betting records mislead almost everyone, including us. A 1–3 night reads like a failure. A 3–1 night reads like genius. Neither tells you much after four tries. The number that matters is whether teams priced at +173 clear the bar more than 36.6% of the time over a season, and you cannot see that in an evening.

Which is a slightly deflating thing to say on a site that publishes nightly picks. But it is the truth, and a board that only made claims you could verify by Wednesday morning would be a board making much weaker claims.

What would actually worry us

  • Run lines never hitting. Not a 1–3 night — a 6–30 month. That would say the distribution assumption is wrong, not unlucky.
  • Every pick landing in one market. Sixteen picks and six run lines is a lean. Sixteen picks and fourteen run lines would mean the model had stopped having opinions about anything else.
  • The edges getting suspiciously large. A 4-point edge on a run line is plausible. A 20-point edge usually means a bug, a stale price, or a market nobody else is bothering to price.
The short version. Most of our run-line picks are bets we expect to lose. They are on the board because losing 59% of the time at +173 still makes money, and they are worth less than they look because they are all the same idea wearing six different uniforms. Judge them in months, not evenings.
